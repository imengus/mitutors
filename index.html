Our statement

We are two medical students, from the University of Bristol and UCL, offering personalised, specialist preparation for UK medical school interviews.

Why we set this up

Generic online material and tutoring can only take a candidate so far, and rarely help them stand out. Tutors at larger agencies often work with many students at once, usually over a short period.

We set up MItutors to offer something more specialist. We take on fewer students and give each one our time. Our single focus: helping them perform as well as they possibly can, at interview and beyond.

How we teach

Each student receives tailored sessions that go beyond what general guides can offer. We explore interview topics in real depth, from medical ethics and the NHS to communication skills and motivation for medicine.

Our aim is for students to walk confidently into their interviews understanding the material, rather than reciting it, so they can reason clearly through any question they are asked.

Why us

Between us, we have interviewed at 8 medical schools and received 6 offers. We both scored in the top 6% of the UCAT, and we bring over 450 hours of teaching experience, from small-group tuition to one-to-one GCSE and A-Level Maths.

We value the relationships we build with our students. As we progress through our own training, we hope to stay in touch, offering mentorship, guidance through medical school, and advice on specialty portfolios and research publications.

How We Got Into Medical School

Medical school interviews can seem daunting, but congratulations, you are at the last step. Like your predicted grades, the UCAT and your personal statement, medical interviews take time to prepare for. You may muddle your words at first, feel unsure about what to say, or not know how to structure your answers to pick up marks on the mark scheme. Rest assured, with time and intentional practice, your marks will improve.

There are three main things to work on:

  1. Build your knowledge base

    A general knowledge base worth learning: key topics that may come up in the interview and are worth being clued up on, plus standard questions you need examples ready for, drawn from your own experiences.

  2. Learn to think and structure

    Learning how to think through questions and structure your answers for the different question types, with an understanding of what the examiners are looking for.

  3. Be ready on the day

    Practical advice on how to feel ready:

    • What to do the night before and the morning of
    • What to say if you want to take a second before answering
    • Techniques to stay relaxed under pressure
    • Having a tidy room
    • Looking after your health (sleep, exercise etc.) so you feel 100% on the day

Mete

So what was it like for me? I knew very little to begin with. I started watching YouTube videos and reading online articles to familiarise myself with the medical interview process.

I set up a Word document and wrote short summaries of what I thought was valuable to know, as well as examples from my personal experiences geared towards the more behavioural questions I might be asked. I also wrote down key phrases and a few relaxation and delay techniques I could use to gather my thoughts and smooth out my speech.

Once the basic knowledge was there, I began practising interview questions with family and friends to get better at structuring my answers out loud.

This was the hardest part: turning notes into coherent, structured speech whilst ticking off the examiners’ mark scheme. Alchemists spent centuries trying to turn lead into gold. I spent weeks trying to turn written bullet points into spoken sentences, with similar early results. Fortunately, unlike the Alchemists, I eventually struck gold and received my offers. You may not feel the most confident at this stage, but over time your answers will improve.

Once my answers were respectable, I began practising with medical students from the universities I was applying to, since they could provide insight that students from other schools may not be able to. This is where repetitions and tailored feedback matter for confidence, fluidity of speech and the quality of your answers.

It is also the stage where we’d recommend coming to us, so you get the most out of your money and our time together. Before then, we can provide guidance and study materials, and recommend resources to help you get there. Examiners can tell very quickly which students have put in the time. With good practice, it will be obvious to them that you have.

It also helps to simulate the interview format of the schools you are interviewing at and the types of questions they may ask by doing mock MMIs and panel interviews. This is like sitting a past paper under exam conditions, and is the highest-quality form of revision you can get.

When it came to the interviews themselves, mine at Bristol, Liverpool and Leicester were online, and Sheffield was in person. The in-person one was a lot scarier. You can see the competition and everyone is on edge, a bit like the staredown before a boxing fight.

An anecdote

Not every answer will go well, and that’s fine. At Sheffield I was asked what I was looking forward to about living in Sheffield as a student. I blanked and said visiting the steel factories. As student pastimes go, it is not a common one. In an MMI, each station is usually marked separately, so one weak answer doesn’t decide the whole interview. Leave it behind and move on to the next station.

Get as much experience as possible, and study for it in the same way you study for your exams. Don’t be demoralised if you’re shaky to begin with. It takes time, and we are here to support you every step of the way.

Ilkin

During my application, I found the medical interviews to be what needed the most out-of-the-box preparation. Back then, there was the BMAT exam (UCAT but harder) at the end of October, so I only found time to prepare much later. And yet, this was not an issue! With the proper guidance and organisation, one can grow in confidence day by day as they absorb the information and get fluent in their articulation.

I learned English later in life, so when I first started recording myself for interview practice, I could see I was a bit robotic and unnatural. It gets better, by the end I was totally relaxed and natural. To this I owe many hours of thinking out loud, practising with friends, and receiving detailed feedback from a special teacher who mentored me in my interview process.

However, what consumed more of my time was the knowledge. Given I was applying for Oxford, I wanted my knowledge to be impressively deep and comprehensive. I found some ethics textbooks online (e.g. Medical Ethics: a Very Short Introduction), visited my local university to read the British Medical Journal etc, searched up medical law and tribunal procedures, and read up further on key cases such as Gillick or Bolam.

I also read lots of BBC/ Guardian health, watched loads of YouTube of doctors describing their daily work and how their team functions, and reflected thoroughly on my own work experience to find talking points.

There can’t be any cutting of corners, so during this period I had to be honest with myself that I was not slacking off. Yet, to be honest the main thing was consistency rather than intensity. A few hours every day spread out over months meant that with time I felt I had gone beyond expectations and was working at a level that would impress interviewers.

My hope is that you too can find the right strategies to move past mediocrity because, unfortunately, no matter how virtuous or compassionate you are, only those that can stand out are given a place. Thankfully, I know that whichever student receives the right guidance and sticks to the right regimen will be able to get to the same confidence.

I really thank my early Dec Oxford interview for this, because preparing at that level meant that my next interviews March onwards for UCL/ Southampton/ King’s went like a breeze.

An anecdote

For my MMI UCL interview I had to stay in my friend’s student accommodation in London on the bare floor! I had less than 3 hours sleep, was immensely underdressed compared to the other candidates, and yet the sole reason why it went well was the undeniable comprehensiveness of my preparation. Rather than me putting on a special performance, my preparation shone through naturally.

The same with my group-interview at Southampton. I decided I would reapply rather than go to Southampton, so I preferred to just joke around unseriously, and yet when the interviewers asked me the serious questions, when I opened my mouth the level of my preparation spoke for me. That was an accidental offer!

All this to say you need not worry about how you will perform in an interview, as it all happens automatically to the level of your preparation and confidence.

I know that this is probably the most unpredictable time of your application. It may feel like you are left to your own devices, trying to meet a standard and yet retain your originality. No need to be crushed by the burden, as long as you enjoy the process of exploration and deepening. I’m here to make that process easier and give the essential feedback that assures you that you’re on the right track.
