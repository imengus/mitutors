Our statement

We are two medical students, from the University of Bristol and UCL, offering personalised, specialist preparation for UK medical school interviews.

Why we set this up

Generic online material and tutoring can only take a candidate so far, and rarely help them stand out. Tutors at larger agencies often work with many students at once, usually over a short period.

We set up MItutors to offer something more specialist. We take on fewer students and give each one our time. Our single focus: helping them perform as well as they possibly can, at interview and beyond.

How we teach

Each student receives tailored sessions that go beyond what general guides can offer. We explore interview topics in real depth, from medical ethics and the NHS to communication skills and motivation for medicine.

Our aim is for students to walk confidently into their interviews understanding the material, rather than reciting it, so they can reason clearly through any question they are asked.

Why us

Between us, we have interviewed at 8 medical schools and received 6 offers. We both scored in the top 6% of the UCAT, and we bring over 450 hours of teaching experience, from small-group tuition to one-to-one GCSE and A-Level Maths.

We value the relationships we build with our students. As we progress through our own training, we hope to stay in touch, offering mentorship, guidance through medical school, and advice on specialty portfolios and research publications.

Our strategies

Over our experience, we learned three main things:

  • 1 · Knowledge
  • 2 · Articulation
  • 3 · Habits

1Knowledge

Know deeper and broader than other candidates

Read things that feel profound to you. Interviewers will take notice.

  • Medicine is very deep. Only by stimulating your curiosity can you begin to understand beneath the surface.

  • You may start from the basics before going deeper: YouTube and articles talk about interview process

  • Make sure to check out doctors' and patients' lived experiences on YouTube so you gain second-hand experiene.

  • Make structured notes on what you learn and group them by question topic. The way you learn the knowledge is not different to how you would study for an exam.

  • Going back to my volunteering and work experiences, I dissected and reflected in depth to find talking points.

  • It's very useful to read (and digest): basic medical ethics textbooks and articles (BMJ/ Guardian Health etc), landmark cases (Gillick/ Bolam/ Re C etc), and tribunal proceedings against malpractising doctors.

  • Research all aspects of the university, the course, and your career progression.

  • You must enjoy the process of exploration and deepening, otherwise you will burn out.

2Articulation

Learn to think deeply on the spot

An good interview answer is always reasoned from scratch.

  • This is the hard part: turning notes into coherent, structured speech whilst ticking off the examiners’ mark scheme.

  • Practise your answers out loud and record them. Notes that read well don’t always sound right when spoken.

  • Initially you may need a lot of time to think and craft your responses, but over time you will become surprisingly good at answering without thinking. We want you to be a natural.

  • Unless if you receive detailed feedback, you cannot know what level you are working at. Either you will be overconfident, or you will underestimate your strength. Both are a tragedy in an interview.

  • If you put in the time, it will be difficult to hide your competence.

  • Mock MMIs and panel interviews are like sitting a past paper under exam conditions, and the highest-quality form of revision you can get.

  • Find students from your desired medical schools or those that have gone through the interview process to practise with.

  • This is the stage where we’d recommend coming to us, so you get the most out of your money and our time together.

3Habits

Build healthy habits in advance of the day

Confidence and soft-skills stabilise with long-term habits.

  • First guidance, then consistency in your practice and lifestyle. Build yourself up, and the rest is automatic.

  • There can’t be any cutting of corners, so during this period hold yourself and ask others to hold you to a high standard. Stick to a weekly schedule that demands regular output.

  • Get as much experience as possible, and study for it in the same way you study for your exams.

  • Don’t be demoralised if you’re shaky to begin with. It takes repeated effort to develop your soft-skills, and we are here to support you every step of the way.

  • Meaningful social contact, regular sleep and exercise are as important as your preparation. After all, you need to feel well to show your competence.

  • Plan how to feel ready on the day, what to do the night before and the morning of, and what to say if you want to take a second before answering.

  • Don’t cram the night before. Get your things ready and get a good night’s sleep.

  • Have a tidy room, and look after your health (sleep, exercise etc.) so you feel 100% on the day.
