Our strategies

We can roughly group our wisdom into three areas:

  • 1 · Knowledge
  • 2 · Articulation
  • 3 · Habits

1Knowledge

Know deeper and broader than other candidates

Dive into areas that feel profound to you. Interviewers will take notice.

What to do

In our words

Start from the basics

  • YouTube videos and articles outline the interview process
  • Create a big-picture to-do list so you don't miss anything
  • Interview variations and types of questions: PanelMMI
  • Types of questions: e.g. data-interpretationroleplaySJT

Read further and digest

  • Hot topics and key debates: e.g. abortionstrikesAIageingprevention
  • Knowledge of the NHS: e.g. NHS structure + bodiescare division + MDT
  • Knowledge of the profession: e.g. GMCcareer progression specialties
  • Read articles every day to get a sense of how the profession thinks: BMJKing's FundGuardian Health
  • Landmark cases to tie into your answers: e.g. GillickBolamRe C
  • MPTS Tribunal proceedings against malpractising doctors. You can link examples of doctors getting struck off.
Ilkin

Medicine is very deep. The more I got curious about its depth, the more satisfaction I felt in going deeper. Without stimulating my curiosity, my brain wouldn't have had the focus to think deeper.

Reflect on your own experience

  • So much depth and detail that can be uncovered from your major life experiences
  • There are more talking points to be found in your volunteering and work experience
  • What are your strengths and weaknesses/ shortcomings?
  • Have your own view on the key debates, because examiners want to hear your reasoning, not a summary of the news.
Mete

Keep a document with short summaries, key topics, and examples from your own experience. A few strong examples can cover most behavioural questions.

Know the university and the course

  • Are there inspiring lecturers or researchers?
  • How is the course structured, and what are its strengths? Beyond the prospectus.
  • Student facilities and wellbeing, student societies
  • The city, surroundings, hospitals, local projects
Mete

Examiners can tell very quickly which students have put in the time and which haven’t. With good practice, it will be obvious to them that you have.

Use YouTube for second-hand experience

  • Doctors narrating their daily plan and incidents
  • Doctors’ views on the shortcomings of medicine and the NHS
  • Patients: what are their biggest (dis)satisfactions?
  • Lived-experiences of patients that really touch you, so your reasons for medicine are emotionally grounded
  • Recorded ward rounds and consultations to get a feel for it.
Ilkin

YouTube is good for placing yourself in doctors' or patients' shoes. Then, in an interview, your answers will have noticeable substance.

Make structured notes

  • Group by topic so they are easily accessible
  • Learn key facts and mnemonics like you would for an exam
  • There is so much to learn, prioritise making notes on what you know the least

2Articulation

Learn to think deeply and structure answers on the spot

A good interview answer is always reasoned from scratch.

What to do

In our words

Learn the structure of an answer, not the answer

  • Using Point Evidence Explain for opinion-based questions and Situation Task Action Result Reflection for experience-based ones
  • What examiners look for: their marking criteria
  • Learning what makes a quality answer: Genuine On-point Logical Detailed
Mete

Practise your answers out loud. Notes that read well don’t always sound right when spoken.

Practise on your toes

  • First answer without your notes, then incorporate them in a new answer
  • Record your answers, and give very honest feedback to yourself
  • During your timed practice, ask family/ friends to put you in unfamiliar situations
  • Use unpredictable questions, so you learn step by step reasoning on the spot
  • Even if you are preparing a script for a question, get used to saying it in a new way each time
Ilkin

Even if it is cringe-inducing, you must self-analyse with painstaking honesty. If you don't address your shortcomings, then who will?

Practise with people that will give you real feedback

  • Students from (your desired) medical schools
  • Those teachers and older relatives that can give you the harsh truth
  • Those with good life experience can give feedback on how you appear as a human
  • Those experienced in running mocks at your school or online
  • Receiving trustworthy feedback gives you confidence on what you do know, and helps you quantify how much you don't know
Ilkin

Unless you receive detailed feedback, you cannot know what level you are working at. Either you will be overconfident, or you will underestimate your strength. Both are a tragedy in an interview.

Simulate the real thing

  • Mock MMIs and panel interviews in each school’s format
  • The more the better, as long as it pushes you unexpectedly and is under timed conditions
  • Over time, it just feels like sitting a past paper under exam conditions
Ilkin

By my fourth interview, I was a lot more relaxed and natural.

Mete

Get as much experience as possible, and study for it in the same way you study for your exams.

3Habits

Build practical and healthy habits in advance of the day

Confidence and soft-skills stabilise with long-term habits.

What to do

In our words

Set your standard high and keep your rhythm constant

  • No cutting corners: hold yourself, and ask others to hold you, to a high standard
  • A weekly schedule that demands regular output
  • A few hours every day, spread over months really adds up.
  • Read your notes and learn cumulatively. Don't cram into the last days.
Ilkin

Maintaining a consistent few hours a day, you will end up working at a level that you couldn't have predicted.

Mete

Don’t be demoralised if you’re shaky to begin with. It takes time, and we are here to support you every step of the way.

Keep a healthy and balanced lifestyle

  • If your mind is agitated, or if you are feeling heavy with pessimism, you cannot engage at your best
  • Keeping stable and well absorbs the impact of things going wrong (before or during the interview)
  • Anxiety can be good to motivate you to sacrifice your time, but your lifestyle needs to keep it in check
  • Regular sleep and exercise for grounded thinking and controlled anxiety
  • Interviewers don't appreciate a robot/ chatbot. Meaningful time with friends and family helps keep your human side alive

Make looking through a wise mindset habitual

  • To get into your desired med school, you don't need to be the best (impossible). You just need to satisfy certain requirements
  • Relieve the pressure: you are choosing to undergo this stressful ordeal, and no one will be harmed if you don't get in the first time
  • Have a realistic outlook: I am not guaranteed a place, but I will work humbly to do what I can
  • It's not sustainable to be motivated by fear: of failure, of losing one's identity, or losing opportunities
  • Remember you are fallible. It is better for your confidence to expose your hidden weaknesses than to run away from them.
  • View the interviewers as nobly upholding the quality of the medical profession, which is why you must rise to meet them. Medicine would degrade if it reduced the standards of its doctors.
Ilkin

Those with wise insights are calmer, more realistic in outlook, and better able to perform stably.

Make your technique automatic

  • Pause before answering
  • Begin thinking along PEE and STARR approaches
  • Speak slower and keep composure
  • Key phrases, plus relaxation and delay techniques to gather your thoughts
Mete

Keep a phrase ready to buy a second: “That’s an interesting question, let me take a second to consider it.”

Plan the day itself

  • Accept that there is little that you can do. Stay present, and face whatever comes.
  • On the day itself, cramming will actually be harmful to your mental balance.
  • Rule: There will be at least one thing that goes wrong.
  • As long as you feel 60% confident, it usually works out
  • Even better with a good night’s sleep and a tidy room
  • Look after your health so even if one thing goes wrong, you can face the rest
Ilkin

The night before my UCL MMI I had to sleep on my friend's floor so I got no sleep. What got me the offer were 2 things: my level of preparation that did the speaking for me, and the fact I was in a good space in the previous weeks.

Mete

At Sheffield I was asked what I was looking forward to about living in Sheffield as a student. I blanked and said visiting the steel factories. Not to worry, interviewers give more weight to certain mistakes than a simple slip. I moved on and got my offer!
